Modelling materials.
Building descriptors.

My modelling work connects experimental characterization with thermal, photothermal, and electrochemical response. I use MATLAB to process measurements, fit physical models, construct interpretable descriptors, and examine how material structure enters a mathematical description.

The framework follows Ti₃AlCN MAX, acid-etched Ti₃CNTₓ, and DMSO-treated Ti₃CNTₓ through complementary measurements of structure, surface chemistry, porosity, dispersion, and time-dependent response.

01 / MODELLING THE MEASUREMENT

Characterization is part of the model.

My modelling begins with the experimental signal itself. Diffraction profiles, vibrational spectra, chemical envelopes, adsorption measurements, and dispersion data require different mathematical treatments before they can become useful descriptors.

Baseline correction, peak fitting, band integration, and physically defined transformations provide a traceable connection between raw measurements and the quantities used in subsequent analysis.

XRD — diffraction profiles and stacking descriptors

X-ray diffraction profiles are processed through baseline estimation and peak-profile fitting. Gaussian, Lorentzian, or pseudo-Voigt functions provide descriptions of selected reflections, depending on the profile being examined. Single- and multiple-component descriptions can be compared through residuals and information criteria.

Icorrected(2θ) = Iraw(2θ) − Ibaseline(2θ) d = λ / (2 sin θ) Lc = Kλ / (β cos θ)

Extracted descriptors: peak position, basal spacing, full width at half maximum, integrated peak area, apparent coherent stacking length, and a normalized parent-region diffraction index.

Here β is the peak width expressed in radians for the coherence calculation. When instrumental broadening is not removed, Lc is treated as an apparent coherence descriptor. It does not directly represent particle size, individual flake thickness, or a measured layer count.

The parent-region index tracks intensity within a selected precursor-related region. Its interpretation remains distinct from quantitative phase analysis or a direct calculation of etching efficiency.

Raman — vibrational shifts and line-shape modelling

Raman peak fitting extracts center frequency, linewidth, and integrated area for selected spectral features. Comparisons focus on corresponding vibrational features, with attention to changes in peak overlap and mode identity across the material series.

Apeak = ∫ I(ω) dω Δω = ωsample − ωreference Relative linewidth = Γsample / Γreference

Extracted descriptors: mode-specific frequency shifts, fitted linewidths, relative broadening, and selected spectral area ratios.

A normalized Raman-shift variable supplies the vibrational descriptor used in the composite framework. Linewidth-based quantities provide a separate description of spectral broadening. Absolute intensity is considered cautiously because it also depends on acquisition conditions.

FTIR — baseline treatment and band-area descriptors

FTIR processing combines polynomial baseline correction, Savitzky–Golay smoothing, and integration over defined spectral windows. Broad, overlapping features are represented through operational band areas rather than assigning each shoulder to an independent chemical species.

Bi = ∫[ν1 to ν2] Acorrected(ν) dν Rsurface = B2 / B1 Rdefect = B3 / B1 ROH = B4 / B1

XPS — constrained chemical-envelope modelling

XPS modelling separates survey composition from high-resolution chemical-envelope analysis. Background alternatives, component line shapes, and linked spin–orbit partners constrain the interpretation of overlapping spectral contributions.

Candidate sulfur envelopes are assessed against background-only descriptions and control samples. Treatment history is therefore kept separate from spectral evidence for a particular retained chemical species.

Extracted descriptors: quantifiable elemental ratios such as F/Ti, relative chemical-envelope component fractions, and TiOₓ-like contributions.

Survey atomic composition and fitted component fractions have different meanings. A component's share of an individual envelope is not an independent atomic percentage, and a value below the reporting threshold is not automatically assigned a numerical zero.

Gas sorption — porosity and effective transport models

Surface area and pore-volume measurements support geometric descriptors of accessible interfaces and porosity. Effective-medium calculations then explore how pore fraction and interface resistance enter a thermal transport model.

Dp ≈ 4Vp / SBET φ = Vp ρbulk av = SBET ρbulk keff = kporous / (1 + Rint av kporous) aeff = keff / (ρeff cp,eff) eeff = √(keff ρeff cp,eff)

Descriptor inputs: specific surface area SBET, pore volume Vp, and operational pore diameter Dp. Derived quantities include assumed-density pore fraction φ and volumetric interface area av, using consistent units.

A Maxwell–Eucken treatment supplies a porous-medium conductivity term. An interface-resistance contribution extends this description to account for the influence of interfacial area.

Conductivity, diffusivity, and effusivity calculated through this framework are assumption-dependent model outputs. They remain separate from directly measured material descriptors.

Zeta potential and DLS — dispersion descriptors

Zeta-potential magnitude supplies an electrokinetic measure, while dynamic light scattering provides an operational hydrodynamic size. Together, these quantities support dispersion descriptors that account for both charge-related behavior and aggregate size.

Electrokinetic descriptor = |ζ| Size transformation = log10(Dh) Provisional combined index = |ζ| / Dh

Extracted descriptors: zeta-potential magnitude, hydrodynamic diameter, normalized size, and combined dispersion measures.

For sheet-like materials, hydrodynamic size can reflect dispersed aggregates. It is not interpreted as a direct measurement of lateral sheet dimensions. A polydispersity correction is not calculated when PDI is unavailable.

02 / DESCRIPTOR ARCHITECTURE

Give every predictor a physical meaning.

Characterization-derived variables are organized into interpretable families before response modelling. The framework distinguishes structural and interfacial information, vibrational changes, and dispersion behavior.

The asterisk denotes normalization across the study's material set. These scores are comparative coordinates within that framework, rather than universal material constants. Constant-valued inputs require exclusion or a separately defined treatment because their min–max denominator is zero.

Descriptor screening before fitting

Screening considers missing values, reporting thresholds, physical equivalence, acquisition sensitivity, redundant transformations, and dependence on assumed parameters. Alternative FTIR measures, pore descriptors, and size transformations provide sensitivity checks.

Concentration as an explicit model variable

Material descriptors and concentration represent different aspects of the experiment. Concentration therefore enters response models explicitly. Linear, logarithmic, power-law, and saturation forms provide alternative mathematical descriptions.

Example descriptor-based structure: Qnet = β0 + βC ln(C / C0) + βS S Alternative concentration model: Qnet = Qmax C / (KC + C)

C0 is a reference concentration that makes the logarithm dimensionless. These expressions show the modelling structure without displaying fitted coefficients or experimental outcomes.

03 / NON-ISOTHERMAL KINETICS

Model thermal conversion event by event.

Thermogravimetric and derivative-thermogravimetric signals define conversion intervals, event boundaries, and characteristic temperatures. Event-specific normalization allows individual mass-loss processes to be examined through kinetic descriptions.

α(T) = [m0 − m(T)] / (m0 − mf) dα/dT = (A / β) exp[−Ea / (RT)] f(α) β = dT/dt

Here α is conversion within the selected event, β is heating rate, A is an apparent pre-exponential factor, and Ea is apparent activation energy.

The kinetic framework uses Kissinger, Flynn–Wall–Ozawa, Kissinger–Akahira–Sunose, Starink, and Friedman treatments. These approaches examine peak-temperature behavior and conversion-dependent kinetics through different mathematical formulations.

Kinetic descriptors

Event mass fraction, onset temperature, DTG peak temperature, conversion-dependent apparent activation energy, and apparent kinetic parameters.

Event reconstruction

Effective reaction-order descriptions reconstruct selected conversion intervals. Their interpretation depends on event separation, heating-rate coverage, and consistency across conversion windows.

Derivative sensitivity and overlapping transformations are examined before attributing a fitted kinetic description to a particular physical process.

04 / PHOTOTHERMAL HEAT BALANCE

Separate heat input from heat loss.

Time-dependent heating and cooling are represented through a lumped energy balance. The model connects effective thermal capacity, heat input, and environmental heat loss to the measured temperature transient.

Cth dΔT/dt = Qin − hA ΔT ΔTheating(t) = ΔT∞ [1 − exp(−t / τH)] ΔTcooling(t) = ΔToff exp[−(t − toff) / τC] hAwater = Cth,water / τC,water Qnet,off = hAwater (ΔTsample,off − ΔTwater,off)

Separate heating and cooling time constants describe the experimental transients. A water reference establishes a consistent heat-loss calibration for calculating reference-corrected net heat input.

Transient descriptors

Temperature-rise amplitude, heating time constant, cooling time constant, reciprocal time constants, transient area, net heat input, and mass-specific heat input provide complementary descriptions of the response.

Keep the thermal quantities distinct

The temperature at laser switch-off is distinguished from an extrapolated asymptotic temperature. Heat-balance estimates are also distinguished from intrinsic photothermal conversion efficiency, which requires absorbed optical power.

Residual structure and temporal autocorrelation are examined during uncertainty assessment, because neighboring temperature observations may not provide independent information.

05 / ELECTROCHEMICAL RESPONSE

One material. Several measurement timescales.

Cyclic voltammetry, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, and galvanostatic charge–discharge provide complementary observations of charge storage, resistance, and relaxation. Each technique retains its own model structure, acquisition checks, and parameter definitions.

CV — branch separation and scan-rate scaling
ibg(V) = [ia(V) + ic(V)] / 2 is(V) = [ia(V) − ic(V)] / 2 is(V, ν) = a(V) ν^b(V) is(V, ν) = k1(V) ν + k2(V) ν^(1/2)

Anodic and cathodic branches support separation of background and symmetric current contributions. Power-law scaling and two-term rate decomposition examine current dependence at comparable potentials.

Model quantities: loop-integrated capacitance, scan-rate exponent b, and the operational coefficients k1 and k2. Comparisons require consistent potential windows, cycle selection, and acquisition conditions.

EIS — equivalent circuits and distributed relaxation
ZCPE(ω) = 1 / [Q (jω)^n] τRC = RC τR–CPE = (RQ)^(1/n) C*(ω) = 1 / [jω Z(ω)]

Candidate circuits describe series resistance, capacitive branches, non-ideal constant-phase behavior, and conductive pathways. Frequency-dependent complex capacitance supplies a complementary representation of the impedance response.

Model quantities: resistance parameters, CPE coefficient Q, exponent n, characteristic relaxation times, and effective capacitive measures.

Linear Kramers–Kronig checks, residual structure, and parameter identifiability support model assessment. Q is not interpreted directly as capacitance when n differs from one.

GCD — charge balance and current-dependent capacitance
Ccharge = I Δtcharge / ΔV Cdischarge = I Δtdischarge / ΔV q = ∫ I dt E = ∫ V I dt

Resolved charge and discharge segments provide apparent capacitance, charge balance, and energy-based measures. Potential-window definitions and resistive voltage changes are treated explicitly during current-dependent comparisons.

Constant, power-law, and finite-limit response forms provide candidate descriptions of how apparent charge storage changes with measurement rate.

State-space simulation and cross-technique modelling
dx/dt = Ax + bI V = cᵀx + DI

Finite RC networks are expressed in state-space form to simulate piecewise voltage ramps and constant-current segments. This provides a bridge between circuit parameters and time-domain electrochemical signals.

A constant-phase element has no exact finite-dimensional state-space representation. It therefore remains mathematically distinct from a finite RC realization.

Circuit alternatives, candidate rate laws, bootstrap uncertainty, and withheld scan rates, currents, or frequencies support assessment across measurement techniques.

06 / COMPUTATIONAL PRACTICE

MATLAB as the modelling workspace.

MATLAB connects signal processing, parameter extraction, descriptor construction, and physical simulation within a traceable computational workflow.

  1. Import and preserve. Retain raw measurements together with material identity, units, acquisition settings, and processing choices.
  2. Process the signal. Apply baseline treatment, smoothing, event segmentation, and quality checks appropriate to each measurement.
  3. Extract interpretable parameters. Fit diffraction and spectral profiles, integrate bands, reconstruct thermal transients, and estimate kinetic or electrochemical parameters.
  4. Construct the descriptor table. Distinguish measured quantities, fitted parameters, derived ratios, composite scores, and assumption-dependent model outputs.
  5. Compare model structures. Examine residuals, information criteria, parameter stability, descriptor sensitivity, and dependence between observations.
  6. Verify the implementation. Check units, limiting behavior, reconstructed signals, and agreement between analytical expressions and numerical simulations.
